summary: 'The article discusses the use of predictive simulations based on explicit, physiologically inspired control policies to test theories on motor control and evaluate interventions on different components of control. Several control architectures have been proposed for simulating locomotor tasks, such as fully feedback or reflex-based controllers or feedforward mimicking Central Pattern Generators (CPGs). Hybrid architectures combining both feedback and feedforward components represent a viable alternative to these existing controllers.

The article presents a new hybrid controller for predictive simulations of cycling where the feedforward component is integrated into the control policy. This approach allows the simulation to generalize across different tasks, potentially improving its utility in various applications such as robotics, biomechanics, or human motor learning. The authors argue that this design enhances the generalizability of neurophysiological principles behind feedback-based controllers while maintaining their core benefits.

The article highlights the limitations of existing controller-based simulations and proposes a solution by proposing a new hybrid controller for predictive simulations of cycling tasks. This innovative approach aims to address the current challenges in simulating complex motor control tasks across different domains.'

by Giacomo Severini, David Muñoz Predictive simulations based on explicit, physiologically inspired, control policies, can be used to test theories on motor control and to evaluate the effect of interventions on the different components of control. Several control architectures have been proposed for simulating locomotor tasks, based on fully feedback, reflex-based, controllers, or on feedforward architectures mimicking the Central Pattern Generators. Hybrid architectures integrating both feedback and feedforward components represent a viable alternative to fully feedback or feedforward controllers. Current literature on controller-based simulations almost exclusively presents task-specific controllers that do not generalize across different tasks. The task-specificity of current controllers limits the generalizability of the neurophysiological principles behind such controllers.
